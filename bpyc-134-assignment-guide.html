BPYC-134 Western Philosophy: Modern: how to study for the assignment

A study map for BPYC-134 Western Philosophy: Modern: the four official blocks, how to read arguments, handle names and terms, compare positions fairly and check accuracy.

Start with your own assignment paper

Before you read anything else, open the official Current Assignments page and search for BPYC-134. Match your programme, medium and the session the assignment is valid for, then download the paper. Read the questions, any choice pattern, the word limits and the submission instructions in that PDF. Confirm the deadline and how to submit from the official notice that applies to you.

A similar course name or a store label is not proof that a paper is the right one. Compare the exact code with your own enrolment record. Then write a checklist of every part of every question, and note which unit each part seems to belong to before you start writing.

Read modern philosophy as an argument

BPYC-134 is Western Philosophy: Modern. The best way to study it is to read each position as an argument: what is the claim, what reasons support it, and what is the objection. A list of names and dates will not help much when a question asks you to explain or compare. Aim to be able to say, in plain sentences, what a view holds and why its supporters hold it.

For each unit, write a short note with three lines: the main claim, the main reasons, and the main criticism the unit mentions. Add the unit reference. These notes become the base for any answer, and they keep you close to your course text.

The four blocks as a reading map

The official eGyanKosh page for BPYC-134 lists four blocks: Precursor to Modern Western Philosophy; Rationalism; Empiricism; and Critical and Dialectic Philosophies. These are block titles, not the unit-by-unit syllabus and not a list of expected questions. Match each question to the unit it needs. A block name appearing here does not mean a question will come from it.

In the precursor block, keep background separate from the main arguments. In Rationalism and Empiricism, write down the course text's definitions, positions and the distinctions it draws between them. Do not treat "Critical and Dialectic Philosophies" as one label for views that are the same. Check which thinker, issue or argument each unit actually discusses. Write about a doctrine or a claim only after reading it in your unit, and avoid adding a famous quotation from memory.

Reduce confusion over names and technical terms

Make a glossary with each term, its meaning in the course context and the unit reference. If a term has different translations in different sources, follow the wording of your official material and your allowed medium, and use it consistently. You can keep the English spelling beside it. You do not have to replace every difficult word with an everyday word. Keep the term accurate and explain it in simple sentences.

Do a separate pass for attribution. Read your draft and ask of each claim: is this the thinker's own claim, a critic's comment or my summary? If you cannot tell, go back to the source. If you use another philosophy's vocabulary to explain a view, check that the meaning has not changed. Words that sound alike are not always the same concept. A short internet summary is not reliable only because it is short.

Choose a structure to match the question

For "explain", open with the idea, then unpack the reasoning step by step, and end with a line that sums up the point. For "compare", pick a few shared dimensions, such as what each view says about the source of knowledge, and treat both views on each dimension. For "critically examine", present the view fairly first, then the objections and the replies the unit mentions, and end with a limited judgement.

If a question has several parts, keep a heading or a clear paragraph break for each. Do not spend most of the answer on background that the question did not ask for. A biography is useful only when the question needs it.

A practice outline for a comparison (hypothetical)

For practice, imagine a question that asks you to compare two views on how knowledge comes about. This is not from any assignment. Make an outline: the shared question both views answer; view A in two or three points with the reasons given in your unit; view B in the same way; two or three dimensions on which they differ; one fair criticism of each; and a closing line that states what the comparison shows.

Leave the content boxes empty until you have read the units, then fill them from the text. If a box stays empty, read again. Do not fill it from memory of a quotation. Your real answer is built from your own reading.

Reconstruct arguments in your own words

Read a passage, then write the argument as numbered steps: premise one, premise two, conclusion. Close the book and explain it aloud. Compare with the text and fix any step you changed. Keep the technical terms, and explain them in sentences of your own. If you notice you can only repeat labels, go back to reading.

When you present an objection, state it in its strongest fair form. A weak one-line version of the view you disagree with makes a comparison biased. You may disagree with a view, but you must first show that you understand it.

Be fair in comparison and criticism, then do an accuracy pass

Give each view a similar amount of space and a fair statement. Separate the criticism that the course text mentions from your own comment, and label your own comment as yours. Avoid saying one view is "simply wrong". Say what it fails to explain and why, according to the argument.

Use a short routine: map the questions, read the units, write argument notes, draft, then run an accuracy pass for names, terms and attributions. Keep a list of doubts to resolve with your course material and academic support. Any private study material is an optional help, and it can contain errors.

Do I have to write the philosopher's biography?

Only if the question needs it. Give background only when it helps explain the argument. Most answers are better with a focus on the claim, the reasons and the objections.

Is using the book's technical words copying?

No. Technical terms from your course material can be used. What matters is that you explain them and the reasoning around them in your own sentences. Put exact quotations in quotation marks with their source.

Can I write the current assignment from an old paper?

No. Old papers may differ in questions and instructions. Use your current official assignment as the base. Earlier material can help you practise a method, but it does not replace the current paper.
