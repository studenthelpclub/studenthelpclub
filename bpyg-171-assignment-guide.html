BPYG-171 Applied Ethics: how to study for the assignment

A study map for BPYG-171 Applied Ethics: the three official blocks, separating facts from assumptions, linking claim, reason and objection, and common mistakes to avoid.

Start with your own assignment paper

Before you read anything else, open the official Current Assignments page and search for BPYG-171. Match your programme, medium and the session the assignment is valid for, then download the paper. Read the questions, any choice pattern, the word limits and the submission instructions in that PDF. Confirm the deadline and how to submit from the official notice that applies to you.

A similar course name or a store label is not proof that a paper is the right one. Compare the exact code with your own enrolment record. Then write a checklist of every part of every question, and note which unit each part seems to belong to before you start writing.

Turn an opinion into a reasoned answer

BPYG-171 is Applied Ethics. Having a strong personal opinion about an ethical issue and explaining it with academic reasoning are two different skills. You are not asked to announce what is right or wrong. You are asked to examine what the question is about, which principles are relevant, and what reasons different positions give, using your course material. The focus of the answer should be the argument, not personal judgement or a social media debate.

A reading notebook with four columns can help: issue, position, supporting reason and objection. If a case has two views, first record both in fair language. Giving your preferred view a detailed treatment and the other a weak one-liner makes the comparison biased. You may disagree, but you must first understand what you disagree with.

The three blocks as a study map

The official eGyanKosh page for BPYG-171 lists three blocks: Introduction to Applied Ethics; Ethical Debates; and Introduction to Professional Ethics. These are broad topic groups, not the full syllabus and not a list of expected questions. For each real question, find the unit title and pages that apply. Do not include material from a unit your question does not touch just to make the answer longer.

In the introduction block, make clear notes on terms and the scope of the subject. In the debates block, keep the boundary of each issue and the competing reasons apart. In the professional ethics block, use points on role, responsibility and conflict only when the unit and the question discuss them. If you refer to a particular professional rule or a current law, check the applicable source. Do not turn a course discussion into legal advice or into today's rule for every profession.

Keep facts and assumptions apart

If the question gives a case, note the facts that are supplied. Do not add a missing fact just because it helps your conclusion. If an assumption is needed to continue, say it is an assumption and explain how the reasoning would change if it were different. If you use a real-life example, remove details that identify private people. Do not claim that someone had a bad intention or did wrong without evidence.

A hypothetical example is a safe way to practise, as long as you label it as hypothetical. Do not present a realistic-sounding story as a real incident. In your case notes, list the stakeholders and the decision you are analysing, and note each one's interest or responsibility instead of only writing names. Not every ethical issue calls for the same framework, so check the course context first.

Connect claim, reason and objection

After you write a main claim, ask: what course-based reason supports it? After the reason, ask: how does it connect to the case or the question? Then look at a relevant alternative view or objection. An answer is long in a useful way when the analysis moves forward. Repeating the same moral judgement in different words is not analysis. Let the conclusion come out of the reasoning in the body, and do not force a decided result into every paragraph.

If the question asks you to compare, use shared headings. If it asks you to explain, clarity of the concept comes first. If it asks you to discuss critically, add relevant objections and limits to the description. Adjust the scope to the exact instruction, and check the word limit and submission instructions in your paper.

A practice outline for a professional dilemma (hypothetical)

For practice, imagine a hypothetical professional decision in which two responsibilities conflict. This is not a question from any assignment and not a ready answer. In the first part of the outline write the boundary of the conflict and the known facts. In the second, add placeholders for the course concepts that seem relevant. In the third, write the reasons for position A and its limits. In the fourth, the same for position B, and in the fifth, a comparison of what interest or responsibility each position puts first.

Fill the content only after you have read your units. Finish with a carefully limited conclusion and note what remains uncertain. If you cannot decide without another fact, saying so honestly is better than forcing a verdict. Put a unit reference in a box beside each reason.

Own-word writing: explain the reasoning, not the labels

After a passage, explain the concept from your notes to an imaginary friend. If you can only repeat a label or a definition, go back to the reading. Draft with the book closed, then compare your meaning with the book. A technical term can stay the same, but the sentences and examples should come from your understanding. Keep exact quotations short, in quotation marks and with their source.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using a moral slogan in place of an argument.
  • Mixing up law and ethics. Something can be legal and still be debated ethically, and the reverse, so say which one the question is about.
  • Writing only your opinion and no reason from the course.
  • Presenting an opposing view in a weak form.
  • Adding facts to a case that were not given.
  • Naming a stakeholder without saying what is at stake for them.

Use a review pass that checks each of these. Keep unresolved doubts in a list and clear them with your course material and academic support. Any private study material is optional, and it can have errors.

Can I write my own opinion in the assignment?

Yes, if the question allows it, but support it with reasons from your course material and show that you understand other views. An opinion without reasoning is not enough for an academic answer. Check the exact wording of the question.

Do I have to give a real incident as an example?

No. A well-explained hypothetical example, clearly labelled, can be enough if it fits the question. If you use a real incident, check the facts, leave out private details and do not make claims without evidence.

Will these topics definitely come in my assignment?

No. The block titles are only for organising your study. Check the real questions, instructions and validity in the official assignment PDF. This page does not predict questions and does not guarantee marks or acceptance.
