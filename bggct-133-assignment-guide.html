BGGCT-133 General Cartography: how to study for the assignment

A study map for BGGCT-133 General Cartography: the five official blocks, how to read a map question, checking scale and units, and presenting maps and diagrams clearly.

Start with your own assignment paper

Before you read anything else, open the official Current Assignments page and search for BGGCT-133. Match your programme, medium and the session the assignment is valid for, then download the paper. Read the questions, any choice pattern, the word limits and the submission instructions in that PDF. Confirm the deadline and how to submit from the official notice that applies to you.

A similar course name or a store label is not proof that a paper is the right one. Compare the exact code with your own enrolment record. Then write a checklist of every part of every question, and note which unit each part seems to belong to before you start writing.

Cartography rewards process, not memory

BGGCT-133 is General Cartography. In a subject like this, how you work matters as much as what you remember. A map question usually asks you to read, measure, draw or interpret something. If you jump straight to a final drawing or a final number, small slips in scale, units or labelling can spoil the whole answer. Slow down and write down what is given, what is asked and what method your unit teaches for it.

Keep a notebook where you write each method as steps in your own words, with a note of the unit it comes from. When you meet a new question, find the matching method first. Work with your own course book and your own tools, and treat any other source as a way to check your understanding, not as something to copy.

The five blocks as a reading map

The official eGyanKosh page for BGGCT-133 lists five blocks: Introduction to Cartography; Map Projections; Sources of Data; Map Reading and Interpretation; and Representation of Data. The map-reading block also lists units on topics such as topographical maps, representation of climatic data and weather maps. These are reading leads, not a statement that your assignment will ask about all of them. Match each question in your paper to the unit it really belongs to.

Keep notes by block. For the introduction, note the key terms. For projections, note what each projection is for and what it keeps or distorts, as your unit describes it. For sources of data, note what kinds of source exist and what limits each has. For reading and interpretation, keep a list of the steps for each kind of map. For representation of data, note the choices you must make about symbols, classes and layout.

A disciplined way to read a map question

First, list what is provided: the map or table, its scale, the units, the date or source if given. Second, mark what is asked, usually with action words such as "measure", "calculate", "draw", "describe" or "interpret". Third, decide which unit and method apply. Fourth, plan the answer: the steps you will show and what the final result will look like.

When you describe a pattern on a map, say only what the map shows. Do not add causes the map cannot tell you. When you interpret, separate what you can see from what you are inferring, and label an inference as an inference. If the question gives a map extract and you are tempted to use a map from the internet, check that it matches the extract in scale, area and date before you rely on it.

Make a habit of checking scale, units and working

Write the scale at the top of any measurement answer, in the form your unit uses. Write the units on every number. When you convert between a map distance and a ground distance, show the step, then check the result for sense: is a village road really that long? Do the calculation twice, once forward and once as a check in the opposite direction, if the method allows it.

A final number without working is hard to trust, and for you it hides where a slip might be. Show your steps even when the paper does not ask for them. Keep your rounding consistent and say how you rounded. If your two attempts disagree, find the difference before writing the final version.

A practice outline for a map-reading answer (hypothetical)

For practice, imagine a question that gives a small map extract and asks you to describe the main features and interpret the settlement pattern. This is not a question from any assignment. Make an outline of six parts: what is given (scale, area, symbols); what the question asks; the features you can see, listed from the map only; the pattern you can describe from those features; a limited interpretation, labelled as an interpretation; and a short closing line.

Under each part, add a placeholder for evidence: which symbol, grid reference or unit supports it. If a part has no evidence, go back to the map and your unit instead of guessing. You can do this exercise on any map from your course book. Your real answer must be your own work, on your own paper.

Diagrams and maps: clarity before decoration

A clear map or diagram has a title, a scale or a statement that it is not to scale, a key, and labels that can be read. Use symbols and line styles consistently, and make sure they match the key. Keep colours simple and distinct. If you shade areas, check that the shading order makes sense, for example light to dark for low to high, as your unit teaches.

A beautiful drawing with a wrong scale is a wrong answer. Check the outline and positions against the data before you ink. If the paper does not ask for a map, do not add one only to make the answer look longer. If it does, make sure the text and the drawing say the same thing.

Write it in your own words and review common mistakes

Explain each method as if you were teaching a classmate, then compare with the book. Check that a technical term has not changed meaning in your wording. Typical mistakes are a missing unit, a mixed-up scale, labels that cover features, an interpretation presented as a fact, and a map from another area. Make a list of the mistakes you tend to make and check for them in the final pass.

Use a simple routine: map the questions, read the units, write method notes, practise on a hypothetical case, draft, then review. Any private reference material is optional, and it can have errors or gaps like any other source.

Does every question need a map?

No. Draw a map only when the question asks for one or when it clearly helps the explanation. Read the instruction and the marks. A drawing that adds nothing does not improve the answer.

Can I use a map from the internet?

Only if it matches what the question needs, and only as a check or a source you have noted. Compare scale, area, date and symbols with your question. Do not replace the map given in your paper with a different one. Always say which source you used.

Will these topics definitely come in my assignment?

No. The block titles are only for organising your study. Check the real questions, instructions and validity in the official assignment PDF. This page does not predict questions and does not guarantee marks or acceptance.
